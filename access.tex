\begin{abstract}
Despite all the institutional support, growth of the infrastructure for incubating early-stage businesses, and the record fundraising of startups in India, the startup failure rate remains high, with around 70\% of startups failing during the first five years. Although innovation capability has been identified as a main reason for startups' failure, most of the empirical evidence is based on viewing innovation as a single concept, which neglects the various aspects of product, process, and marketing innovations that contribute to the success or failure of startups. This study fills this gap by developing a model that stems from Dynamic Capabilities Theory and Resource Based View (RBV) of the firm and considers Innovation Capability (IC) as a multidimensional construct consisting of product, process, and marketing innovations. We examine direct and indirect effects of IC on Startup Survival (SS) with the mediating variable Competitive Advantage (CA); and identify Founder Experience (FE) and Funding Stage (FS) as critical boundary conditions that moderate the effects of IC on SS. The proposed model is examined by employing Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) on the data sample of 200 Indian technology startup founders who are registered with DPIIT. The results offer strong empirical evidence for all direct, mediated and moderated pathways, showing that Innovation Capability directly supports survival prospects, but this impact was strongly mediated by the pathway of competitive advantage. In addition, the returns on innovation to survival are significantly boosted by both the stage of the innovation at which the venture enters the market, and founder experience. The study ends with practical recommendations for entrepreneurs, investors, and policy makers, providing a structured self-diagnostic tool to help early-stage businesses overcome challenges to survival.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ==========================================================

\PARstart{S}{omebody} launches a tech start-up in Bengaluru, Mumbai or Hyderabad today in the nominally 3rd largest technology startup ecosystem. But with the odds aren't playing in your favour. Around 70\% of startups in India fail within 5 years of their establishment and for various high growth tech industries, the ratio of failure is even greater \cite{b1}. Despite all this, startup failure rates have not fallen in step with the increase in capital. In fact, despite record investment — VC funding in Indian startups exceeded USD~42 billion in 2021 — failure rates have not dropped commensurately. There is a contradiction that needs explaining.

A startup's innovation capability is the most commonly used explanatory variable in startup survival studies. The idea is clear: if a business can harness intuition to create a product offering that is differentiated, continually improve its operations, and adjust its market strategies, they are more likely to withstand the competition of others and the environment. This has been widely cited in the literature, e.g. in \cite{b4, b5}. What makes it tricky is that many studies try to measure innovation as a net score, which hides the complexity of innovation. Product, process and marketing innovations are not synonymous. They utilize different organizational resources, have a different risk profile and respond differently to market conditions \cite{b6}. A new product development focused startup with poor process discipline may run out of runway before they get to market size, while a process discipline focused startup that rushes to get its business processes in place may lose the agility they need to get to market or stay there. Thus, separating out the innovation construct is not a methodology nicety, but rather it alters substantive conclusions.

Another drawback of the previous research is geographic. Most of the empirical research on the survival of new ventures and innovation has come from North America or Western Europe \cite{b7}. Institutional context is important in non-trivial ways - access to formal credit, supplier and distribution networks, regulatory predictability, attitudes towards failure, etc. affect how innovation capabilities affect survival outcomes. The lessons learnt in Silicon Valley or the Mittelstand in Germany are less easily transferable to markets in which founders are often less wealthy with collateral to support debt financing and a single regulatory approval can take months of organisational effort.

A third gap is in terms of mechanism. The correlation between innovation capability and survival, while important, does not necessarily provide an explanation for how this correlation is formed. Superior value in relation to the options available has been suggested as the intervening construct \cite{b8} but this mediation hypothesis has yet to be tested empirically with the Indian start-up firms at the beginning stages. Little systematic research has been done on the role of founder expertise and funding resources in the innovation–survival relationship in this context either \cite{b9}.

This research tries to fill the gaps within a single integrated framework, based on Dynamic Capabilities Theory \cite{b10} and the Resource Based View (RBV) \cite{b11}. In the primary survey of 200 founders of start-ups registered with DPIIT, six research hypotheses are developed and tested using PLS-SEM. We analyze the effect of multidimensional Innovation Capability (IC) on startups' survival, the possible mediation by competitive advantage, as well as the individual effects of experience of the founders and the stage of funding received. The literature review, which can be found in Section~\ref{sec:lit}, is based on theoretical and empirical research. In Section~\ref{sec:framework} the conceptual model is explained and the research hypotheses developed. The research design and analytical procedure are described in Section~\ref{sec:method}. The empirical findings are presented in Section~\ref{sec:results} and the implications of the findings in the theoretical and practical aspects are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit}
% ==========================================================

\subsection{Innovation and Startup Survival}

Schumpeter's conception \cite{b12} of the process of capitalist development as a periodic series of wave-like shifts from innovation to inefficiency and from inefficiency to innovation, away from the centre of the mainstream, brought innovation to the core of competition. It has generally been accepted over the last seventy years at the firm level that those firms that invest in innovation tend to live longer, and grow faster, than those firms that do not invest in innovation \cite{b4}. It has been slower to emerge from this research what sort of innovation is important, and when.

Oslo Manual categorizes product, process and marketing innovation as analytically separable activities, which a firm can independently or simultaneously engage in \cite{b3}. In the startup world, this distinction is not to be taken lightly. Product innovation involves a long-term R&D investment and customer facing experimentation; process innovation requires operational discipline in the inside; and marketing innovation requires go to market creativity and customer data infrastructure, all of which are needed to support the other innovations. For a start-up team that is resource-constrained with a limited number of employees and a limited amount of money, these activities have real trade-offs, and they have real consequences. However, most empirical studies on innovation capability for survival are either based on a composite index of the dimensions, or base their study entirely on product innovation, ignoring the effects of process and marketing innovation.

\subsection{Dynamic Capabilities and Startup Performance}

Teece, Pisano, and Shuen \cite{b10} developed a framework called Dynamic Capabilities to explain that firms would be able to maintain advantage when the competitive environment is changing more rapidly than a firm's static stocks of resources are able to respond. The three micro-level processes are at the core of the framework: sensing (scanning), seizing (reconfiguration) and reconfiguring (transforming). The three processes are all running on a 24/7 basis for startups: Product roadmap changes, revenue model goes through iterations, and the founding team goes through restructuring. Dynamic capabilities offer the theoretical language to explain the conditions that enable and/or constrain the ability of some ventures to make it through these transitions and others to fail \cite{b13}.

Amongst all the processes, Eisenhardt and Martin \cite{b14} noted an important distinction: in very dynamic markets, these processes tend to boil down to discernable best practices instead of being strictly firm-specific and idiosyncratic. Their rewording comes in handy here. PIC and PROC can be seen as observable, benchmarkable dynamic capabilities (easily measured with survey tools and that can be compared between a set of startups) as this study requires.

\subsection{Innovation Ecosystems and Social Capital}

There is no such thing as an innovation without competition, and a company's environment acts as a catalyst which determines whether internal strengths are converted to market value. Social capital is the aggregate of network relationships, shared norms and interpersonal trust that enable coordinated action \cite{b15} and it has two-fold relevance when considering start-up innovation. The structural characteristics of dense networks are referrals, warm introductions and early-adopter access. Cognitively, they are able to spread tacit knowledge, which is hard to articulate and teach.

Being a startup environment, India has seen significant transformations since 2016. The Startup India programme established a formal infrastructure for recognition by the DPIIT; Tier-1 incubators like NSRCEL Bengaluru, T-Hub Hyderabad and CIIE Ahmedabad have professionalised early-stage support; and a growing and limited-partner base has taken VC deeper into rounds that were out of reach for most Indian founders \cite{b2}. It is an empirical open question whether these improvements have an additional impact on the effect of firm-level innovation capacity on survival or whether they can be regarded as compensation for a reduced innovation capacity within the firms.The moderating hypotheses in Section~\ref{sec:framework} are based on an empirical question: Do these improvements reinforce the impact on survival of the firm's innovation capacity or do they rather replace a weaker capacity?

\subsection{Resource-Based View and Competitive Advantage}

The deceptively simple idea that underlies Barney's \cite{b11} Resource-Based View is that performance variation among firms in the same industry is due to differences in the resources that they possess, which are strategically valuable and imperfectly mobile. A resource is to have the "VRIN" properties that have become a terminology in strategic management; it must be valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable \cite{b16}.

The capability for innovation meets these requirements when it's not in the personal skills of a few people or the precise instructions of a few protocols, but is part of organisational habits and tacit knowledge. A start up in which innovation is an inherent part of the culture, iterative processes and norms, and growing customer insight is much more difficult to replicate than one in which the key to success is a single algorithm or registered patent. However, the link between the RBV and entrepreneurship has been drawn explicitly by Alvarez and Busenitz \cite{b17} who suggest that the opportunity discovery and exploitation of opportunities is itself a VRIN resource, the defining characteristic of entrepreneurship. In the survival case, the suggestion is that if the innovation capacity is a real VRIN asset for the startup, it will outperform the other startups in their class that are able to more easily replicate this capability.

\subsection{Competitive Advantage as a Mediating Mechanism}

DCT and the RBV do not define a direct link between the capability and the survival of the firm, but rather a link to the competitive advantage as the intermediate step. The argument is: Capability is the source of advantage, which is the source of performance, which is the source of survival. Empirical studies often skip this link and directly regress survival on ability measures without going through the mechanism \cite{b4,b5}. The intermediate variable is then not sufficiently validated.

A partial piece of evidence is offered in the case of Spanish manufacturing firms by Camisón and Villar-López \cite{b18} who show that the innovation capability affected firms' performance via their competitive position. Their study is valuable, but in two ways: (1) They focus on "established" firms for which competitive advantage is relatively stable, whereas the context of Indian tech startups is still finding a "product-market fit"; and (2) while the context is European manufacturing, which also has differences from that of Indian tech startups. This study seeks to determine whether the mediated pathway is in effect when competitive advantage is contested and temporary.

\subsection{Founder Characteristics as Boundary Conditions}

The nature of the results of identical innovation capabilities is not the same in all startups, as context modulates effect. Founder experience and funding stage are two of the most theoretically-based moderators.

On the institutional side, Colombo and Grilli \cite{b19} found in a longitudinal study of new-technology based firms in Italy that experienced entrepreneurs (those that had worked in their industry before founding a business) were more likely to build their companies at a much faster rate than inexperienced entrepreneurs, but this was not necessarily because experienced entrepreneurs were more innovative; it was because they were more efficient at transforming innovation into commercial products. The pathway is judgment: wise founders are more likely to know the buying habits of customers, the dynamics of the competition, and the right moment to enter the market. This moderation effect is something that needs to be empirically examined in the Indian tech space where investors always look at the quality of the founders as the top criterion.

The funding stage is available in another stream. External capital loosens the resource limitations that otherwise dampens the innovation--survival relationship of the early-stage businesses \cite{b20}. You can hire for a seed-funded startup, but a founder team must hire on their own bandwidth. Hellmann and Puri \cite{b20} found that venture capitalists helped their startups become more professional, in line with the hypothesis that better capitalized startups derive more survival value from their innovation efforts than do those that are bootstrapped.

\subsection{Research Gaps and Study Positioning}

Based on this review, there are five gaps that are connected. The relationship between innovation ability and survival has not been quantitatively studied for the Indian start-ups to date. The independent impact of PIC, PROC and MIC on outcomes of survival have not been measured. The early-stage venture population has never been examined in terms of the mediating role of competitive advantage. Prior studies have not tested founder experience or funding stage as moderators in a structural model in this setting. Additionally, there is no framework that has been developed in the Indian context of Startups which combines these constructs by using PLS-SEM. The model discussed in Section~\ref{sec:framework} directly tackles each gap.

\section{Conceptual Framework and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:framework}
% ==========================================================

\subsection{Conceptual Framework}

The proposed framework, depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}, is a combination of the Dynamic Capabilities Theory and the Resource Based View (RBV) into one explanatory framework. Innovation Capability (IC) is defined as a multidimensional construct that is measured through three different dimensions: Product Innovation Capability (PIC), Process Innovation Capability (PROC) and Marketing Innovation Capability (MIC). This is a multidimensional construct that is used as the main independent variable. Competitive Advantage (CA) is seen as the mediator that will link the ability of innovation to the ultimate outcome of interest, Startup Survival (SS). Finally, Founder Experience (FE) and Funding Stage (FS) act as moderators to affect the relationship between innovation capability and start-up survival. Path directions and potential signs are deductively inferred based on the strategic management theory and empirical entrepreneurship literature.

\subsection{Hypothesis Development}

\textit{Innovation Capability and Competitive Advantage:}
As per the Resource Based View (RBV), the base of any competitive advantage of any firm is its unique capabilities. A critical strategic asset is the firm's ability to be innovative, that is, to continuously adapt and offer new products, processes and promotions. Combining product, process, and marketing adaptation can enable startups to provide unique value to customers, cut down on operational costs, and position themselves in a unique place in the market. These actions create competitive advantage against competitors, through a defensible market position. Therefore, we hypothesize:

\begin{description}
  \item[H1:] Innovation Capability (IC) has a significant positive effect on Competitive Advantage (CA).
\end{description}

\textit{Competitive Advantage and Startup Survival:}
The ability of the firm to create and capture greater value is its competitive advantage. Once startups have taken a competitive foothold in a market, either by differentiating, specializing in a niche, or positioning themselves strategically, they become less vulnerable to severe macro-economic changes and strong competition in the marketplace. This strategic insulation is what ultimately contributes to the stabilization of revenue, customer retention and long-term viability, the lifeblood of a venture's survival in a dynamic market. Thus:

\begin{description}
  \item[H2:] Competitive Advantage (CA) has a significant positive effect on Startup Survival (SS).
\end{description}

\textit{Innovation Capability and Startup Survival:}
While capabilities to innovation and innovation to performance is mediated by competitive advantage, according to dynamic capabilities theory, there is also a direct link from innovation to survival. The ability to continuously innovate is crucial for organizational agility to pivot business models, adjust to regulatory changes and respond to unforeseen environmental shocks in highly turbulent start-up situations – before the changes even become a stable competitive position. Consequently:

\begin{description}
  \item[H3:] Innovation Capability (IC) has a significant positive effect on Startup Survival (SS) directly.
\end{description}

\textit{The Mediating Role of Competitive Advantage:}
Combining the RBV and dynamic capabilities, we suggest that the survival advantages of innovation are not purely direct, but is also greatly mediated by the competitive advantage that is created. To establish a niche in a competitive market, startups need to convert the innovativeness of the idea into marketable competitive positions, be it through providing differentiated products or saving on costs. So we anticipate competitive advantage to be a key intervening process:

\begin{description}
  \item[H4:] Competitive Advantage (CA) mediates the relationship between Innovation Capability (IC) and Startup Survival (SS).
\end{description}

\textit{The Moderating Roles of Founder Experience and Funding Stage:}
There are certain foundation and venture-level boundary conditions that restrict the effect of innovation capability on start-up survival. First of all, there is founder human capital, including industry experience, that is needed to effectively commercialize innovative activities. Veteran entrepreneurs can use strategic heuristics to help innovation ventures reduce waste and speed up their time to market. Secondly, the stage of external funding is indicative of resource availability. Ventures that progress from bootstrapping and angel rounds into seed and venture capital funding phases benefit from the financial cushion needed to professionalize operations, scale innovations, and absorb the risks associated with failure. We believe that the two factors increase the returns to innovation in survival:

\begin{description}
  \item[H5:] Founder Experience (FE) positively moderates the relationship between Innovation Capability (IC) and Startup Survival (SS).
  \item[H6:] Funding Stage (FS) positively moderates the relationship between Innovation Capability (IC) and Startup Survival (SS).
\end{description}

\section{Research Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
% ==========================================================

\subsection{Research Design}

The study is deductive in nature as it draws hypotheses for testing from the existing theory and empirically tests them with primary data collected from the surveys. To some extent a preference is given to a quantitative, cross sectional design over a longitudinal design, in part because it is difficult to follow up startup cohorts over time, and in part because the data suggest that the likelihood of survival may vary significantly among these failures (see \cite{b21}). The method is based on the positivist paradigm applied in quantitative entrepreneurship research that involves the use of multi-item reflective scales for the operationalization of constructs and second generation multivariate analysis to assess the structural relationship between constructs.

\subsection{Target Population and Sampling}

The target population is defined by three inclusion criteria. The person(s) responding to the question are required to have a founding or co-founding position in the venture. The startup needs to be registered with the Startup India scheme by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT). The time period must be within the last seven years from the date of incorporation, which is DPIIT's own definition of a startup and falls within the early growth phase when the relationship between innovative activity and survival matters most. The sectors are limited to technology-intensive sectors (information technology, fintech, healthtech, edtech, and agritech) in which there is enough variation in innovativeness to estimate structural models.

The respondents will be contacted via the startup incubators, institutional entrepreneurship cells, the alumni networks of accelerators, as well as via LinkedIn outreach. The inclusion criteria are specific, therefore purposive sampling was decided on, and will be complemented by snowball referrals if response rate is low. The sample size of 200 respondents is still quite large as per the hair et al. 10× rule \cite{b22} that suggests a minimum of about 50 usable responses for the present model structure, and allows for subgroup analyses by funding stage categories.

\subsection{Measurement Instrument}

This questionnaire is divided in 5 parts. Part~A gathers information about the respondents' demographic and characteristics about the venture: founding year, sector, current funding stage, previous entrepreneurial and industry experience, and the size of the team. Each of the 3 innovation capability constructs – Parts~B through~D – is measured by 5 items adapted from Romijn and Albaladejo \cite{b6} and Gunday et al.\ \cite{b3} and modified for the context of early-stage ventures. The items for part~E are on the one hand concerned with competitive advantage (four items, adapted from Camisón and Villar-López \cite{b18}) and on the other hand with survival of the start-up (five items operationalised as perceived revenue continuity, customer retention trajectory, and operational longevity compared with those of other firms in the sector); for part~F founder experience is measured by means of three items, and funding stage is treated as a nominal moderator with five ordinal levels: (bootstrapped, angel-funded, seed-funded, Series~A or above, and grant-supported).

The multi-item measures are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from $1=$ ``Strongly Disagree'' to $5=$ ``Strongly Agree.'' Main data collection will be preceded by a pilot study of 20 respondents to determine problematic items, as well as to check the scale properties.

\subsection{Analytical Strategy}

For this study, three reasons are provided for using PLS-SEM instead of CB-SEM: (1) This is an exploratory-predictive model, not only a confirmatory model; (2) several constructs are hypothesized to have non-normal distributions (due to the skewed nature of startups' performance outcomes); and (3) the sample size (n = 200) is relatively small compared to the number of observations typically required for optimal estimation of CB-SEM (n > 400).

The assessment is carried out in the following two-stage sequence that is recommended by Hair et al. \cite{b22}. First one is the “measurement model” and the results of this model should be more than 0.70 for item loadings, more than 0.70 for Cronbach's $\alpha$ and CR, and more than 0.50 for the AVE, which indicates convergent validity. Both discriminant validity and traditional Fornell-Larcker criterion employ the Heterotrait--Monotrait ratio (HTMT $< 0.85$), a more stringent criterion than the traditional Fornell–Larcker.

Evaluation of the structural model is carried out, using PLS bootstrapping with resampling of 5,000 numbers and the calculation of $t$-statistics and 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) for each of the path coefficients (H1-H3). The mediation test for H4 involves bootstrapping the indirect effect's bias-corrected confidence interval and then checking whether it is significant in addition to the direct effect not being significant; if both the indirect effect (CI) and direct effect (CE) are not significant, then the mediation is considered full. Moderation tests are conducted with the product-indicator interaction term method in PLS-SEM, and Cohen's $f^2$ is used to report the effect size: small = 0.02, medium = 0.15, large = 0.35. The Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR $< 0.08$) is used to assess the model fit.

The common latent factor technique will be used as a post hoc method for screening common method variance (CMV) if the single factor test identifies Harman's single factor solution to explain more than 50\% of the total variance \cite{b21}.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

We present the empirical findings from our structural model analysis and predictive machine learning models below. We first outline the demographic makeup of our sample, followed by the validation of our reflective measurement model and the testing of the structural pathways.

\subsection{Sample Profile and Demographics}

\textbf{Demography of the sample:}
Demographic data for the survey sample was examined before measurement and structural model evaluations to determine representativeness and validity. The final sample is made up of 200 early-stage technology startup companies registered with Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT) under the Startup India initiative. The survey was only performed with startup founders, co-founders, or key executive decision makers as a way to ensure the reliability of the responses. The data collection was done with a cross sectional survey method and purposive and snowball sampling techniques with start-ups incorporated in the past seven years. The survey was made available via technology incubators, national entrepreneurship networks, and professional websites and online platforms.

\textbf{Venture Characteristics and Sectoral composition:}
The detailed analysis of the characteristics of the ventures indicates a very young and technology oriented group. In terms of venture age, most of the sample is in their first year of operation, constituting a significant majority (66.5\%). Twenty-three and a half percent of the sample (23.5\%) are startups 1--3 years old, and 10.0\% are 3--7 years old. It is a very appropriate distribution to be used in analyzing the dynamics of survival because it is a distribution of ventures in the most precarious phase of their developmental life, that is, the most susceptible to the liability of newness. When distributed by sector, the sample is dominated by the Tech/Software sector (50.0\%), followed by HealthTech (33.5\%) and FinTech or other technology-enabled services (16.5\%). This is a sectoral breakdown because the drivers of the Indian technology startup ecosystem is majority growth tech startups, which makes the results representative of high-growth tech startups.

\textbf{Founder Profiles and Human Capital:}
The analysis of the founder profiles suggests a mature and highly experienced group of entrepreneurs. The respondents who have more than ten years of industry specific experience are about two-thirds (66.5\%) and the rest (33.5\%) have less than ten years. From the perspective of being an entrepreneur, the sample is split evenly between first-time entrepreneurs (50.0\%) and repeat entrepreneurs (50.0\%). A balance between serial entrepreneurs and novel entrepreneurs is essential to establish the moderating effects of founder experience. The age distribution of the founders is around mid-career professionals, with 25\% (25 to 34) and 25\% (45 to 54) of people being in the younger and older brackets, respectively. Taken together, these demographic parameters form a strong and good sample that gives a solid empirical basis for the following path analysis using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM).


\subsection{Measurement Model Validation}

Evaluation of the reflective measurement model was introduced in order to obtain the statistical rigor of the structural path analysis. The reliability of the individual indicators, internal consistency reliability, convergent validity and discriminant validity of the three major constructs (Innovation Capability, Competitive Advantage, Startup Survival) were assessed in this evaluation.

\textbf{Methodology and Thresholds:}
The reliability of the internal consistency of the items and construct was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha ($\alpha$) and Composite Reliability ($\rho_c$). Cronbach's alpha is the internal consistency of the set of items; it is expressed as:

\begin{equation}
\alpha =
\frac{K}{K - 1}
\left(
1 -
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sigma_{Y_i}^{2}}
{\sigma_{X}^{2}}
\right)
\end{equation}

In this example $K$ is the number of indicator items, $\sigma_{Y_i}^{2}$ is the variance of indicator $i$ and $\sigma_{X}^{2}$ is the total variance of the construct score.

The composite Reliability ($\rho_c$) is more nuanced measure of consistency of internal items, it is computed as:

\begin{equation}
\rho_c =
\frac{
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_i\right)^2
}{
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_i\right)^2
+
\sum_{i=1}^{K}
\left(1-\lambda_i^2\right)
}
\end{equation}

Indicators: where $\lambda_i$ is the standardized loading of indicator $i$ on the latent construct.

Cronbach's alpha and Composite Reliability are considered to be satisfactory if the minimum value is $0.70$. Convergent validity was checked by the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) that reflects the variance explained by the latent construct as compared to the factor variance explained by measurement error. The $\text{AVE}$ is given by:

\begin{equation}
\text{AVE}
=
\frac{
\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_i^2
}{K}
\end{equation}

where $\lambda_i$ is the standardized factor loading of indicator $i$ and $K$ is the number of indicators. The minimum value for the $\text{AVE}$ must be at least $0.50$ or more, which means that the variance of the indicators for the latent construct is greater than $50\%$.

\subsection{Structural Model Analysis and Hypothesis Testing}

\textbf{Discussing Path Analysis and Hypothesis Testing:}

The validation of the measurement model was followed by evaluating the structural model to test the direct hypotheses (H1, H2 and H3). Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression was used to calculate standardized path coefficients ($\beta$), $t$-statistics and two-tailed $p$-values. A non parametric bootstrapping procedure was used with $5,000$ resamples to assess the statistical significance of the structural pathways.

\textbf{Model Specifications:}
Structural equation for the mediator (Competitive Advantage, $CA_s$):

\begin{equation}
CA_s
=
\beta_{IC \to CA}\cdot IC_s
+
\epsilon_{CA}
\end{equation}

The standardized scores of the innovation capability ($IC_s$) are represented by the standardized scores of Innovation capability, the standardized path coefficient between the innovation capability and the CA is denoted by the standardized path coefficient of the innovation capability to the CA ($\beta_{IC \to CA}$), and the residual variance is defined by ($\epsilon_{CA}$).

The Structural Relationship between the independent variable ($IV_x$) and the dependent variable ($SS_s$) with direct and indirect pathways and moderating interaction is set as:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
SS_s
&=
\beta_{IC \to SS}\cdot IC_s
+
\beta_{CA \to SS}\cdot CA_s
+
\beta_{FE}\cdot FE_s
+
\beta_{FS}\cdot FS_s \\
&\quad +
\beta_{IC \times FE}\cdot (IC_s \times FE_s)
+
\beta_{IC \times FS}\cdot (IC_s \times FS_s)
+
\epsilon_{SS}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\beta_{IC \to SS}$, $\beta_{CA \to SS}$, and $\beta_{IC \times FE}$, $\beta_{IC \times FS}$, are the direct path coefficients, $FE_s$ and $FS_s$ are the scores in the standardized scale for founder experience and funding stage respectively, and $\epsilon_{SS}$ is the residual variance.

\textbf{Direct Path Findings:}

The statistical findings support the three direct hypotheses:

\textbf{Hypothesis H1:}
The path from Innovation Capability to Competitive Advantage is positive and highly significant, $\beta = 0.7448$ with $t = 20.63$ and $p < 0.001$. The effect size is very high ($f^2 = 1.247$) indicating that there is a high impact of the startup internal capability to implement product, process and marketing innovations in determining market positioning and differentiation. This result is highly in line with Resource-based View (RBV) which assumes that innovation capability is made up of pieces of resources that can be valuable, scarce, and inimitable (VRIN) and create competitive barriers.

\textbf{Hypothesis H2:}
Competitive Advantage is positively and statistically significantly related to Startup Survival ($\beta = 0.6019$, $t = 14.18$, $p < 0.001$). Results of this large effect size ($f^2 = 0.435$) suggest that direct effects of defensible market position, distinctive customer value, and strategic differentiation on operational longevity and financial performance of a venture are strong.

The direct path from Innovation Capability to Startup Survival is also positive and significant ($\beta = 0.2945$, $t = 6.88$, $p < 0.001$), but with a medium effect size ($f^2 = 0.125$). This direct connection implies that innovation ability gives start-ups the flexibility they need to respond to environmental shifts and thrive -- even before they are translated to a sustainable competitive advantage.

\textbf{Explanatory Power ($R^2$):}

There is high explanatory power of the structural model. The model explains $55.47\%$ ($R^2 = 0.5547$) of the variance in Competitive Advantage, suggesting that over half of the variance in the market differentiation of startups can be explained by their innovation capability. Moreover, the joint model explains a very high share of variance of Startup Survival ($R^2 = 0.7346$); that is, the innovation capabilities, competitive advantage, and the moderating effects of the context matter to survival in the Indian technology start-up ecosystem to a great extent.


\subsection{Mediation and Moderation Analysis}

The mediating role of Competitive Advantage ($CA$) between Innovation Capability ($IC$) and Startup Survival ($SS$) was tested through Mediation Analysis of Competitive Advantage (H4): Innovation Capability Mediate Relationship between Innovation Capability and Startup Survival. The product-of-coefficients method was used to estimate the indirect path coefficient and bootstrapping with $5,000$ resamples was used for validation. The indirect effect of Innovation Capability on Startup Survival through Competitive Advantage showed a positive and statistically highly significant effect ($\beta_{\text{indirect}} = 0.4483$, $t = 11.48$, $p < 0.001$).

Both direct relationship between Innovation Capability and Startup Survival (H3: $\beta = 0.2945$, $t = 6.88$, $p < 0.001$) and indirect pathway (H4) are positive and significant, which means that Competitive Advantage acts as a partial mediator. Variance accounted for (VAF) index (indirect effect/total effect):

\begin{equation}
\text{VAF}
=
\frac{\beta_{\text{indirect}}}
{\beta_{\text{direct}} + \beta_{\text{indirect}}}
=
\frac{0.4483}
{0.2945 + 0.4483}
=
60.35\%
\end{equation}

The results of the analysis in this study indicate that 60.35\% of the impact of innovation capability on startup survival is passed through competitive advantage. This implies that raw innovation capability directly increases a venture's structural adaptability, the bulk of the survival value of which is realized merely once it has been properly made the most of to gain an invulnerable competitive market position.

In this study, we tested the moderating effect of Founder Experience ($FE$) on the direct link between Innovation Capability ($IC$) and Startup Survival ($SS$), as in Hypothesis H5. The interaction between the IVs and the DVs ($IC \times FE$) was modeled using product-indicator approach. The results of the statistical analysis show that the interaction effect is positive, highly significant ($\beta = 0.2319$, $t = 6.49$, $p < 0.001$) and has a small-to-medium effect size ($f^2 = 0.08$).

This positive coefficient indicates that founder experience is an important positive moderating variable, which strengthens the positive effect of the innovation capability on venture survival. From a practical standpoint, the positive correlation between the survival probability of the startups and their innovation capability is much stronger when the founder has more experience in the industry ($>10$ years). This is consistent with the human capital theory which argues that experienced entrepreneurs draw on their cognitive maps, network of customers and strategic heuristics to commercialise technological and process innovations more efficiently and so overcome the typical operational challenges that result in early stage failures.

To assess the moderating effect of the Funding Stage ($FS$) of the venture on the relationship between Innovation Capability ($IC$) and Startup Survival ($SS$), Hypothesis H6 was tested. The interaction term ($IC \times FS$) was likewise positive and statistically significant ($\beta = 0.1452$, $t = 4.15$, $p < 0.001$, $f^2 = 0.08$), supporting H6.

This finding reinforces the findings that investing in more advanced stages of funding (seed, Series A, B) does increase the survival returns of innovation capabilities. Easing resource constraints like those of start-ups. Funded startups have the capital runway and opportune slack to create their product and marketing innovations more professional, hire specialized people, and expand. Besides, external funding cushions against the commercialization failures so characteristic of the highly novel innovations that startups must face in their commercialization process.

\subsection{Theoretical Implications}

The structural path results provide robust empirical validation of Dynamic Capabilities Theory and the Resource-Based View (RBV) within the emerging market context. From the RBV perspective, our findings show that Innovation Capability acts as a valuable, rare, and inimitable (VRIN) asset that generates a competitive advantage. In alignment with Dynamic Capabilities Theory, the significant direct and moderated pathways show that early-stage survival is a dynamic process of sensing and seizing opportunities, where founder experience and funding stage act as essential boundary conditions that dictate the efficiency of capability deployment.

\subsection{Practical Implications for the Indian Startup Ecosystem}

For entrepreneurs in the Indian startup ecosystem, our findings suggest that focusing solely on technological development is insufficient; equal attention must be paid to process and marketing innovations to establish a defensible market positioning. Founders must prioritize achieving a distinct market position rather than relying solely on technological novelty. For venture capital and angel investors, the significant moderating effects suggest that when evaluating startup innovation, equal weight must be given to founder human capital and capital deployment efficiency. Finally, for policymakers managing initiatives like the Startup India scheme, institutional support should not only focus on funding but also on building robust mentoring and incubator networks that enrich founders' experience and help them navigate early-stage survival hurdles.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Summary of Objectives and Approach}

This study investigated how multidimensional innovation capability (product, process, marketing) influences startup survival among Indian early-stage ventures. Recognizing that 70\% of startups fail within five years, we developed an integrated framework grounded in Dynamic Capabilities Theory and Resource-Based View (RBV). Using PLS-SEM analysis on data from 200 DPIIT-registered startup founders and we tested direct pathways, mediation by competitive advantage, and moderation by founder experience and funding stage.

\subsection{Theoretical and Practical Contributions}

\textit{Theoretically}, this study validates that competitive advantage mediates innovation's impact on startup survival (VAF = 60.35\%), demonstrating that innovation alone is insufficient—translation into market differentiation is essential. Founder experience and funding stage significantly amplify this survival benefit, confirming that human capital and financial resources are critical boundary conditions in emerging markets. The high explanatory power ($R^2 = 0.7346$) supports Dynamic Capabilities and RBV frameworks for Indian tech startups.

\textit{Practically}, entrepreneurs must balance product, process, and marketing innovations; investors should prioritize founder experience alongside innovation metrics; and policymakers require ecosystem-building interventions beyond capital deployment. The Gradient Boosting predictive model (R$^2$ = 0.707) enables founders to assess survival probability and identify capability gaps.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Research}

\textbf{Limitations:} The cross-sectional design limits causal inference; sample restriction to DPIIT-registered tech startups reduces generalizability to informal ventures and non-tech sectors; self-reported measures introduce common method variance; geographic concentration in Tier-1/2 cities may not represent rural or smaller-city startups.

\textbf{Future Directions:} Longitudinal cohort studies tracking actual survival/failure over 5--10 years; comparative institutional analysis across emerging markets (Singapore, Kenya, Brazil); qualitative research on founder decision-making; analysis of innovation portfolio combinations and optimal sequencing; outcome-focused research linking innovation types to specific survival milestones.
