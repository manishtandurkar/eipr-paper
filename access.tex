\begin{abstract}
Despite significant institutional support, expanding incubation infrastructure, and record venture capital inflows, early-stage ventures in India face a persistent failure rate, with approximately 70\% of startups ceasing operations within their first five years. While innovation capability is widely recognized as a primary determinant of startup survival, existing empirical work frequently treats innovation as a monolithic construct, obscuring the distinct ways that product, process, and marketing adaptations influence organizational outcomes. This study bridges this gap by proposing an integrated model grounded in Dynamic Capabilities Theory and the Resource-Based View (RBV), conceptualizing Innovation Capability (IC) as a multidimensional construct comprising product, process, and marketing innovations. We analyze how IC influences Startup Survival (SS) both directly and indirectly through the mediating role of Competitive Advantage (CA), while specifying Founder Experience (FE) and Funding Stage (FS) as critical boundary conditions moderating the relationship. The proposed model is tested using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) on a sample of 200 DPIIT-registered Indian technology startup founders. The findings provide robust empirical support for all direct, mediated, and moderated pathways, demonstrating that while innovation capability directly strengthens survival prospects, its effect is heavily channeled through competitive advantage. Furthermore, both founder experience and later funding stages serve as significant positive moderators, amplifying the survival returns of innovation. The study concludes with practical recommendations for entrepreneurs, investors, and policymakers, offering a structured self-diagnostic tool for early-stage ventures to navigate survival hurdles.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ==========================================================

\PARstart{S}{omeone} founding a technology venture in Bengaluru,
Mumbai, or Hyderabad today enters what is nominally the world's
third-largest startup ecosystem \cite{b2}. The odds, however, are
not favourable. Roughly 70\% of Indian startups shut down within five
years of inception, and across high-growth tech sectors attrition
climbs higher still \cite{b1}. Capital is more available than at any
prior point in India's entrepreneurial history---2021 alone saw over
\$42 billion in venture funding flow into Indian startups---yet failure
rates have not declined proportionately. That contradiction warrants
explanation.

Innovation capability is the most frequently invoked explanatory
variable in startup survival research. The intuition is transparent:
ventures able to develop differentiated products, refine operational
processes, and reconfigure market strategies continuously are better
equipped to outlast rivals and environmental shocks \cite{b3}. Broad
empirical support for this claim exists \cite{b4, b5}. The difficulty
is that most studies operationalise innovation as a single composite
score---an approach that conceals more than it reveals. Product
innovation, process innovation, and marketing innovation are not
interchangeable activities. Each draws on different organisational
resources, carries a distinct risk profile, and interacts differently
with market conditions \cite{b6}. A startup that excels at new-product
development but lacks process discipline may exhaust its runway before
reaching market scale; one that over-invests in operational efficiency
prematurely can sacrifice the customer-facing agility on which early
survival depends. Disaggregating the innovation construct is, therefore,
not a methodological nicety---it changes the substantive conclusions.

A second limitation of existing work is geographic. The overwhelming
bulk of empirical studies on innovation and startup survival originates
in North America or Western Europe \cite{b7}. Institutional context
matters in non-trivial ways: access to formal credit, the density of
supplier and distribution networks, regulatory predictability, and
prevailing attitudes toward failure all shape how innovation capabilities
translate into survival outcomes. Findings from Silicon Valley or the
German Mittelstand transfer imperfectly to markets where founders
frequently lack debt-financing collateral and a single regulatory
clearance can consume months of organisational attention.

A third gap concerns mechanism. Establishing that innovation capability
correlates with survival does not, by itself, explain \textit{how} that
relationship operates. Competitive advantage---the capacity to deliver
superior value relative to available alternatives---has been proposed
as the intervening construct \cite{b8}, but the mediation hypothesis
remains empirically untested for early-stage Indian ventures. The
degree to which founder experience and available funding moderate the
innovation--survival relationship has similarly received scant systematic
attention in this setting \cite{b9}.

This study addresses these gaps within a single integrated framework grounded in Dynamic Capabilities Theory \cite{b10} and the Resource-Based View (RBV) \cite{b11}. Six research hypotheses are developed and tested via PLS-SEM using a primary survey of 200 DPIIT-registered Indian startup founders. We examine how multidimensional Innovation Capability (IC) influences startup survival, whether competitive advantage mediates this relationship, and how founder experience and funding stage condition these dynamics. Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews the theoretical and empirical literature. Section~\ref{sec:framework} outlines the conceptual model and develops the research hypotheses. Section~\ref{sec:method} details the research design and analytical strategy. Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} present the empirical results and discuss their theoretical and practical implications.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit}
% ==========================================================

\subsection{Innovation and Startup Survival}

Schumpeter's argument \cite{b12} that capitalist development proceeds
through recurring waves of ``creative destruction'' placed innovation---
not efficiency or scale---at the centre of competitive dynamics. Seven
decades of subsequent research have broadly validated this position at the
firm level: ventures that invest in innovation survive longer and grow
faster than those that do not \cite{b4}. What that research has been
slower to clarify is which \textit{kind} of innovation matters, and under
what conditions.

The Oslo Manual's taxonomy distinguishes product, process, and marketing
innovation as analytically separable activities that a firm may pursue
independently or in combination \cite{b3}. This distinction carries real
weight in the startup context. Product innovation requires sustained
R\&D effort and iterative customer-facing experimentation; process
innovation demands inward-facing operational discipline; marketing
innovation calls for go-to-market creativity and customer data
infrastructure. For a founding team with limited headcount and finite
capital, trade-offs among these activities are genuine and consequential.
Yet most empirical treatments of innovation capability in the survival
literature either collapse these dimensions into a composite index or
focus exclusively on product innovation, leaving process and marketing
effects largely unmeasured \cite{b6}.

\subsection{Dynamic Capabilities and Startup Performance}

The Dynamic Capabilities framework, introduced by Teece, Pisano, and
Shuen \cite{b10}, was developed to explain how firms sustain advantage
when competitive landscapes shift faster than static resource stocks
can respond. Three micro-level processes are central to the framework:
\textit{sensing}---scanning the environment for emerging threats and
opportunities; \textit{seizing}---reconfiguring resources to capture
identified opportunities; and \textit{reconfiguring}---transforming
existing competencies to maintain strategic alignment as conditions
change. For startups, all three processes are in near-constant operation:
the product roadmap pivots, the revenue model iterates, the founding
team restructures. Dynamic capabilities provide the theoretical vocabulary
for understanding why some ventures navigate these transitions successfully
and others do not \cite{b13}.

Eisenhardt and Martin \cite{b14} offered an important qualification: in
highly dynamic markets, these processes tend to converge toward
identifiable best practices rather than remaining wholly firm-specific
and idiosyncratic. Their reformulation is useful here. PIC and PROC can
be understood as observable, benchmarkable dynamic capabilities---ones
measurable through survey instruments and comparable across a startup
cohort---precisely as this study requires.

\subsection{Innovation Ecosystems and Social Capital}

No venture innovates in isolation, and the ecosystem within which a
startup is embedded shapes whether internal capabilities translate into
market value. Social capital---Putnam's \cite{b15} term for the aggregate
of network relationships, shared norms, and interpersonal trust that
enable coordinated action---has a dual relevance to startup innovation.
Structurally, dense networks supply referrals, warm introductions, and
early-adopter access. Cognitively, they accelerate the diffusion of tacit
knowledge that formal training and documentation cannot easily convey.

India's startup ecosystem has undergone substantial change since 2016.
The Startup India programme created a formal DPIIT-recognition
infrastructure; Tier-1 incubators such as NSRCEL Bengaluru, T-Hub
Hyderabad, and CIIE Ahmedabad have professionalised early-stage support;
and a maturing limited-partner base has extended venture capital
deeper into seed-stage rounds that were previously inaccessible to most
Indian founders \cite{b2}. Whether these improvements amplify the
impact of firm-level innovation capabilities on survival---or simply
substitute for weaker internal capabilities---is an open empirical
question that informs the moderating hypotheses in
Section~\ref{sec:framework}.

\subsection{Resource-Based View and Competitive Advantage}

Barney's \cite{b11} Resource-Based View rests on a deceptively simple
claim: performance differences between firms in the same industry
persist because strategically valuable resources are heterogeneously
distributed and imperfectly mobile. For a resource to generate sustained
competitive advantage, it must be valuable, rare, inimitable, and
non-substitutable---the VRIN criteria that have become standard
analytical vocabulary in strategic management \cite{b16}.

Innovation capability satisfies these criteria when it is embedded in
organisational routines and tacit knowledge rather than residing in
individual contributors or replicable procedures. A startup whose
capacity to innovate is inseparable from its decision-making culture,
iterative norms, and accumulated customer insight is considerably harder
to imitate than one whose advantage depends on a single algorithm or
registered patent. Alvarez and Busenitz \cite{b17} drew the
RBV--entrepreneurship connection explicitly, arguing that the
discovery and exploitation of opportunities---the defining act of
entrepreneurship---itself constitutes a VRIN resource. For the
survival question, the implication is that startups whose innovation
capabilities constitute genuine VRIN resources will systematically
outperform those whose advantage can be more readily matched.

\subsection{Competitive Advantage as a Mediating Mechanism}

Neither Dynamic Capabilities Theory nor the RBV specifies a direct
path from capability to survival---both frameworks operate through
competitive advantage as the proximate outcome. The logic is: capabilities
generate advantage; advantage generates performance; performance enables
survival. Empirical studies frequently short-circuit this chain, regressing
survival directly on capability measures and bypassing the mechanism
\cite{b4,b5}. The mediating link consequently remains largely unvalidated.

Camisón and Villar-López \cite{b18} provide partial evidence in a sample
of Spanish manufacturing firms, where innovation capability influenced
performance indirectly through competitive position. Their study is
important but limited in two respects: the sample consists of established
firms for whom competitive advantage is relatively stable; and the context
is European manufacturing, where market structures differ substantially
from those facing Indian tech startups still searching for product-market
fit. Whether the mediated pathway holds when competitive advantage is
transient and contested is the specific question this study pursues.

\subsection{Founder Characteristics as Boundary Conditions}

Identical innovation capabilities do not produce identical outcomes
across all startups---context modulates effect. Two of the most
theoretically grounded moderators are founder experience and funding stage.

On the experience side, Colombo and Grilli \cite{b19} showed in a
longitudinal study of Italian new-technology-based firms that founders
with prior industry-specific human capital grew their ventures
significantly faster---not because experienced founders innovated more,
but because they were more effective at converting innovation into
marketable output. The pathway runs through judgment: experienced founders
better understand customer purchasing behaviour, competitive positioning
dynamics, and the timing of market entry. In the Indian tech sector,
where investor due diligence consistently elevates founder quality as
the primary selection criterion, this moderation effect deserves
empirical scrutiny.

Funding stage operates through a different channel. External capital
relaxes the resource constraints that ordinarily attenuate the
innovation--survival relationship for early-stage ventures \cite{b20}.
A seed-funded startup can hire for execution; a pre-seed team must rely
on founder bandwidth alone. Hellmann and Puri \cite{b20} documented
that venture capital involvement accelerated operational
professionalisation in funded startups---a finding consistent with the
expectation that better-capitalised ventures extract greater survival
benefit from their innovation capabilities than their bootstrapped peers.

\subsection{Research Gaps and Study Positioning}

Five inter-related gaps emerge from this review. Indian startups have
not been the subject of a dedicated quantitative study linking innovation
capability to survival. The disaggregated effects of PIC, PROC, and MIC
on survival outcomes remain unmeasured. The mediating role of competitive
advantage has not been tested for early-stage venture populations. Founder
experience and funding stage have not been evaluated as moderators within
a structural model for this setting. And no PLS-SEM-based framework
integrating these constructs has been developed for the Indian startup
context. Each gap is directly addressed by the model described in
Section~\ref{sec:framework}.

\section{Conceptual Framework and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:framework}
% ==========================================================

\subsection{Conceptual Framework}

The proposed framework, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}, integrates Dynamic Capabilities Theory and the Resource-Based View (RBV) into a single explanatory structure. Innovation Capability (IC) is conceptualized as a multidimensional construct measured via three distinct dimensions: Product Innovation Capability (PIC), Process Innovation Capability (PROC), and Marketing Innovation Capability (MIC). This multidimensional construct serves as the primary independent variable. Competitive Advantage (CA) is positioned as the mediator through which innovation capability translates into the ultimate outcome of interest, Startup Survival (SS). Finally, Founder Experience (FE) and Funding Stage (FS) function as moderators that condition the relationship between innovation capability and startup survival. The path directions and expected signs are derived deductively from strategic management theory and empirical entrepreneurship literature.

\subsection{Hypothesis Development}

\textit{Innovation Capability and Competitive Advantage:}
In line with the Resource-Based View (RBV), a firm's unique capabilities are the fundamental source of competitive advantage. Innovation capability, representing the firm's capacity to continuously adapt and develop novel offerings, workflows, and promotional strategies, serves as a key strategic asset. By integrating product, process, and marketing adaptations, startups can offer unique value to customers, reduce operational costs, and establish a distinct market position. These actions generate a defensible market position relative to rivals, manifesting as competitive advantage. Therefore, we hypothesize:

\begin{description}
  \item[H1:] Innovation Capability (IC) has a significant positive effect on Competitive Advantage (CA).
\end{description}

\textit{Competitive Advantage and Startup Survival:}
Competitive advantage represents the firm's capacity to create and capture superior value. Startups that successfully establish a competitive advantage—whether through differentiation, niche specialization, or strategic positioning—are better insulated from intense market competition and macroeconomic shocks. This strategic insulation directly drives revenue stability, customer loyalty, and long-term viability, which are the main pillars of venture survival in volatile markets. Thus:

\begin{description}
  \item[H2:] Competitive Advantage (CA) has a significant positive effect on Startup Survival (SS).
\end{description}

\textit{Innovation Capability and Startup Survival:}
Although competitive advantage mediates the translation of capabilities into performance, dynamic capabilities theory suggests that innovation also exerts a direct influence on survival. In highly turbulent startup environments, the capacity to continuously innovate provides the organizational agility necessary to pivot business models, adapt to regulatory shifts, and survive unexpected environmental shocks, even before these adaptations solidify into a stable competitive position. Consequently:

\begin{description}
  \item[H3:] Innovation Capability (IC) has a significant positive effect on Startup Survival (SS) directly.
\end{description}

\textit{The Mediating Role of Competitive Advantage:}
Synthesizing the arguments of the RBV and dynamic capabilities, we propose that the survival benefits of innovation are not entirely direct, but are substantially transmitted through the creation of competitive advantage. Startups must translate their underlying innovation capabilities into clear competitive positions (such as differentiated products or cost efficiencies) to successfully survive in the marketplace. We therefore expect competitive advantage to act as a critical intervening mechanism:

\begin{description}
  \item[H4:] Competitive Advantage (CA) mediates the relationship between Innovation Capability (IC) and Startup Survival (SS).
\end{description}

\textit{The Moderating Roles of Founder Experience and Funding Stage:}
The strength of the relationship between innovation capability and startup survival is bounded by key founder- and venture-level boundary conditions. First, founder human capital, particularly prior industry experience, provides the cognitive maps and managerial networks necessary to commercialize innovative efforts effectively. Experienced founders can apply strategic heuristics to guide innovation projects, reducing waste and accelerating time-to-market. Second, external funding stage reflects resource availability. Ventures transitioning from bootstrapping to angel, seed, and venture capital funding levels gain the financial slack required to professionalize operations, scale innovations, and absorb the risks of failure. We argue that both factors amplify the survival returns of innovation:

\begin{description}
  \item[H5:] Founder Experience (FE) positively moderates the relationship between Innovation Capability (IC) and Startup Survival (SS).
  \item[H6:] Funding Stage (FS) positively moderates the relationship between Innovation Capability (IC) and Startup Survival (SS).
\end{description}

\section{Research Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
% ==========================================================

\subsection{Research Design}

The study takes a deductive stance, deriving testable hypotheses from
established theory and subjecting them to empirical scrutiny through
primary survey data. A quantitative, cross-sectional design is adopted
in preference to a longitudinal approach---a trade-off that sacrifices
causal precision for practical feasibility, given the difficulty of
tracking startup cohorts over time and the near-certainty of differential
attrition among failing ventures \cite{b21}. The approach follows the
positivist tradition of quantitative entrepreneurship research, in which
constructs are operationalised through multi-item reflective scales and
structural relationships are assessed through second-generation
multivariate analysis.

\subsection{Target Population and Sampling}

Three inclusion criteria define the target population. Respondents must
hold a founding or co-founding role in the venture. The startup must be
formally recognised by the Department for Promotion of Industry and
Internal Trade (DPIIT) under the Startup India scheme. The venture must
have been incorporated no more than seven years before the survey date---
a ceiling consistent with DPIIT's own statutory definition of a startup
and with the early-growth phase during which the innovation--survival
relationship is most consequential. Sectoral scope is restricted to
technology-intensive industries (information technology, fintech,
healthtech, edtech, and agritech) where innovation capability variation
is sufficiently wide to permit structural model estimation.

Respondents will be reached through startup incubators, institutional
entrepreneurship cells, accelerator alumni networks, and LinkedIn
outreach. Purposive sampling is appropriate given the specificity of
the inclusion criteria; snowball referrals will supplement direct
outreach where response rates prove insufficient. Hair et al.'s
\cite{b22} ten-times rule implies a minimum of approximately 50 usable
responses for the present model structure; the obtained sample of 200 respondents
provides a substantial buffer and enables subgroup analyses across
funding stage categories.

\subsection{Measurement Instrument}

The questionnaire comprises five parts. Part~A collects demographic
data on the respondent and the venture: founding year, sector, current
funding stage, prior entrepreneurial and industry experience, and team
size. Parts~B through~D measure the three innovation capability constructs
using five items each, adapted from Romijn and Albaladejo \cite{b6} and
Gunday et al.\ \cite{b3} with language tailored to early-stage venture
settings. Part~E covers competitive advantage (four items, adapted from
Camisón and Villar-López \cite{b18}) and startup survival (five items
operationalised as perceived revenue continuity, customer retention
trajectory, and operational longevity relative to sector peers); founder
experience is measured using three items, while funding stage is treated
as a nominal moderator with five ordinal levels: bootstrapped,
angel-funded, seed-funded, Series~A or above, and grant-supported.

All multi-item measures use a five-point Likert scale anchored at
$1 =$ ``Strongly Disagree'' and $5 =$ ``Strongly Agree.'' A pilot
study involving 20 respondents will be conducted before main data
collection to identify problematic items and confirm scale properties.

\subsection{Analytical Strategy}

PLS-SEM via SmartPLS 4.0 \cite{b22} is selected over covariance-based
SEM for three reasons specific to this study: the model is
exploratory-predictive in orientation rather than purely confirmatory;
several constructs are expected to exhibit non-normal distributions
given the skewed nature of startup performance outcomes; and the
anticipated sample size, while adequate, falls short of the 400-plus
observations typically recommended for robust CB-SEM estimation.

Assessment follows the two-stage sequence recommended by Hair et al.\
\cite{b22}. The \textit{measurement model} is evaluated first: item
loadings must exceed 0.70; Cronbach's $\alpha$ and composite reliability
(CR) must exceed 0.70; and AVE must exceed 0.50 to establish convergent
validity. Discriminant validity is assessed via the
Heterotrait--Monotrait ratio (HTMT $< 0.85$), a more stringent test
than the traditional Fornell--Larcker criterion.

\textit{Structural model} evaluation proceeds through PLS bootstrapping
with 5,000 resamplings, generating $t$-statistics and 95\% confidence
intervals for each path coefficient (H1--H3). The mediation
test for H4 applies the bias-corrected bootstrap confidence interval
to the indirect effect: significance of the indirect effect alongside
a non-significant direct effect constitutes full mediation. Moderation
tests for H5 and H6 use the product-indicator interaction term method
within PLS-SEM; effect sizes are reported as Cohen's $f^2$ (small:
0.02, medium: 0.15, large: 0.35). Model fit is evaluated using the
Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR $< 0.08$).

Common method variance will be screened using Harman's single-factor
test; where the single-factor solution accounts for more than 50\% of
total variance, the common latent factor technique will be applied as
a post-hoc correction \cite{b21}.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

We present the empirical findings from our structural model analysis and predictive machine learning models below. We first outline the demographic makeup of our sample, followed by the validation of our reflective measurement model and the testing of the structural pathways.

\subsection{Sample Profile and Demographics}

\textbf{Demography of the sample:}
Demographic data for the survey sample was examined before measurement and structural model evaluations to determine representativeness and validity. The final sample is made up of 200 early-stage technology startup companies registered with Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT) under the Startup India initiative. The survey was only performed with startup founders, co-founders, or key executive decision makers as a way to ensure the reliability of the responses. The data collection was done with a cross sectional survey method and purposive and snowball sampling techniques with start-ups incorporated in the past seven years. The survey was made available via technology incubators, national entrepreneurship networks, and professional websites and online platforms.

\textbf{Venture Characteristics and Sectoral composition:}
The detailed analysis of the characteristics of the ventures indicates a very young and technology oriented group. In terms of venture age, most of the sample is in their first year of operation, constituting a significant majority (66.5\%). Twenty-three and a half percent of the sample (23.5\%) are startups 1--3 years old, and 10.0\% are 3--7 years old. It is a very appropriate distribution to be used in analyzing the dynamics of survival because it is a distribution of ventures in the most precarious phase of their developmental life, that is, the most susceptible to the liability of newness. When distributed by sector, the sample is dominated by the Tech/Software sector (50.0\%), followed by HealthTech (33.5\%) and FinTech or other technology-enabled services (16.5\%). This is a sectoral breakdown because the drivers of the Indian technology startup ecosystem is majority growth tech startups, which makes the results representative of high-growth tech startups.

\textbf{Founder Profiles and Human Capital:}
The analysis of the founder profiles suggests a mature and highly experienced group of entrepreneurs. The respondents who have more than ten years of industry specific experience are about two-thirds (66.5\%) and the rest (33.5\%) have less than ten years. From the perspective of being an entrepreneur, the sample is split evenly between first-time entrepreneurs (50.0\%) and repeat entrepreneurs (50.0\%). A balance between serial entrepreneurs and novel entrepreneurs is essential to establish the moderating effects of founder experience. The age distribution of the founders is around mid-career professionals, with 25\% (25 to 34) and 25\% (45 to 54) of people being in the younger and older brackets, respectively. Taken together, these demographic parameters form a strong and good sample that gives a solid empirical basis for the following path analysis using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM).


\subsection{Measurement Model Validation}

Evaluation of the reflective measurement model was introduced in order to obtain the statistical rigor of the structural path analysis. The reliability of the individual indicators, internal consistency reliability, convergent validity and discriminant validity of the three major constructs (Innovation Capability, Competitive Advantage, Startup Survival) were assessed in this evaluation.

\textbf{Methodology and Thresholds:}
The reliability of the internal consistency of the items and construct was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha ($\alpha$) and Composite Reliability ($\rho_c$). Cronbach's alpha is the internal consistency of the set of items; it is expressed as:

\begin{equation}
\alpha =
\frac{K}{K - 1}
\left(
1 -
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sigma_{Y_i}^{2}}
{\sigma_{X}^{2}}
\right)
\end{equation}

In this example $K$ is the number of indicator items, $\sigma_{Y_i}^{2}$ is the variance of indicator $i$ and $\sigma_{X}^{2}$ is the total variance of the construct score.

The composite Reliability ($\rho_c$) is more nuanced measure of consistency of internal items, it is computed as:

\begin{equation}
\rho_c =
\frac{
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_i\right)^2
}{
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_i\right)^2
+
\sum_{i=1}^{K}
\left(1-\lambda_i^2\right)
}
\end{equation}

Indicators: where $\lambda_i$ is the standardized loading of indicator $i$ on the latent construct.

Cronbach's alpha and Composite Reliability are considered to be satisfactory if the minimum value is $0.70$. Convergent validity was checked by the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) that reflects the variance explained by the latent construct as compared to the factor variance explained by measurement error. The $\text{AVE}$ is given by:

\begin{equation}
\text{AVE}
=
\frac{
\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_i^2
}{K}
\end{equation}

where $\lambda_i$ is the standardized factor loading of indicator $i$ and $K$ is the number of indicators. The minimum value for the $\text{AVE}$ must be at least $0.50$ or more, which means that the variance of the indicators for the latent construct is greater than $50\%$.

\subsection{Structural Model Analysis and Hypothesis Testing}

\textbf{Discussing Path Analysis and Hypothesis Testing:}

The validation of the measurement model was followed by evaluating the structural model to test the direct hypotheses (H1, H2 and H3). Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression was used to calculate standardized path coefficients ($\beta$), $t$-statistics and two-tailed $p$-values. A non parametric bootstrapping procedure was used with $5,000$ resamples to assess the statistical significance of the structural pathways.

\textbf{Model Specifications:}
Structural equation for the mediator (Competitive Advantage, $CA_s$):

\begin{equation}
CA_s
=
\beta_{IC \to CA}\cdot IC_s
+
\epsilon_{CA}
\end{equation}

The standardized scores of the innovation capability ($IC_s$) are represented by the standardized scores of Innovation capability, the standardized path coefficient between the innovation capability and the CA is denoted by the standardized path coefficient of the innovation capability to the CA ($\beta_{IC \to CA}$), and the residual variance is defined by ($\epsilon_{CA}$).

The Structural Relationship between the independent variable ($IV_x$) and the dependent variable ($SS_s$) with direct and indirect pathways and moderating interaction is set as:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
SS_s
&=
\beta_{IC \to SS}\cdot IC_s
+
\beta_{CA \to SS}\cdot CA_s
+
\beta_{FE}\cdot FE_s
+
\beta_{FS}\cdot FS_s \\
&\quad +
\beta_{IC \times FE}\cdot (IC_s \times FE_s)
+
\beta_{IC \times FS}\cdot (IC_s \times FS_s)
+
\epsilon_{SS}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\beta_{IC \to SS}$, $\beta_{CA \to SS}$, and $\beta_{IC \times FE}$, $\beta_{IC \times FS}$, are the direct path coefficients, $FE_s$ and $FS_s$ are the scores in the standardized scale for founder experience and funding stage respectively, and $\epsilon_{SS}$ is the residual variance.

\textbf{Direct Path Findings:}

The statistical findings support the three direct hypotheses:

\textbf{Hypothesis H1:}
The path from Innovation Capability to Competitive Advantage is positive and highly significant, $\beta = 0.7448$ with $t = 20.63$ and $p < 0.001$. The effect size is very high ($f^2 = 1.247$) indicating that there is a high impact of the startup internal capability to implement product, process and marketing innovations in determining market positioning and differentiation. This result is highly in line with Resource-based View (RBV) which assumes that innovation capability is made up of pieces of resources that can be valuable, scarce, and inimitable (VRIN) and create competitive barriers.

\textbf{Hypothesis H2:}
Competitive Advantage is positively and statistically significantly related to Startup Survival ($\beta = 0.6019$, $t = 14.18$, $p < 0.001$). Results of this large effect size ($f^2 = 0.435$) suggest that direct effects of defensible market position, distinctive customer value, and strategic differentiation on operational longevity and financial performance of a venture are strong.

The direct path from Innovation Capability to Startup Survival is also positive and significant ($\beta = 0.2945$, $t = 6.88$, $p < 0.001$), but with a medium effect size ($f^2 = 0.125$). This direct connection implies that innovation ability gives start-ups the flexibility they need to respond to environmental shifts and thrive -- even before they are translated to a sustainable competitive advantage.

\textbf{Explanatory Power ($R^2$):}

There is high explanatory power of the structural model. The model explains $55.47\%$ ($R^2 = 0.5547$) of the variance in Competitive Advantage, suggesting that over half of the variance in the market differentiation of startups can be explained by their innovation capability. Moreover, the joint model explains a very high share of variance of Startup Survival ($R^2 = 0.7346$); that is, the innovation capabilities, competitive advantage, and the moderating effects of the context matter to survival in the Indian technology start-up ecosystem to a great extent.


\subsection{Mediation and Moderation Analysis}

The mediating role of Competitive Advantage ($CA$) between Innovation Capability ($IC$) and Startup Survival ($SS$) was tested through Mediation Analysis of Competitive Advantage (H4): Innovation Capability Mediate Relationship between Innovation Capability and Startup Survival. The product-of-coefficients method was used to estimate the indirect path coefficient and bootstrapping with $5,000$ resamples was used for validation. The indirect effect of Innovation Capability on Startup Survival through Competitive Advantage showed a positive and statistically highly significant effect ($\beta_{\text{indirect}} = 0.4483$, $t = 11.48$, $p < 0.001$).

Both direct relationship between Innovation Capability and Startup Survival (H3: $\beta = 0.2945$, $t = 6.88$, $p < 0.001$) and indirect pathway (H4) are positive and significant, which means that Competitive Advantage acts as a partial mediator. Variance accounted for (VAF) index (indirect effect/total effect):

\begin{equation}
\text{VAF}
=
\frac{\beta_{\text{indirect}}}
{\beta_{\text{direct}} + \beta_{\text{indirect}}}
=
\frac{0.4483}
{0.2945 + 0.4483}
=
60.35\%
\end{equation}

The results of the analysis in this study indicate that 60.35\% of the impact of innovation capability on startup survival is passed through competitive advantage. This implies that raw innovation capability directly increases a venture's structural adaptability, the bulk of the survival value of which is realized merely once it has been properly made the most of to gain an invulnerable competitive market position.

In this study, we tested the moderating effect of Founder Experience ($FE$) on the direct link between Innovation Capability ($IC$) and Startup Survival ($SS$), as in Hypothesis H5. The interaction between the IVs and the DVs ($IC \times FE$) was modeled using product-indicator approach. The results of the statistical analysis show that the interaction effect is positive, highly significant ($\beta = 0.2319$, $t = 6.49$, $p < 0.001$) and has a small-to-medium effect size ($f^2 = 0.08$).

This positive coefficient indicates that founder experience is an important positive moderating variable, which strengthens the positive effect of the innovation capability on venture survival. From a practical standpoint, the positive correlation between the survival probability of the startups and their innovation capability is much stronger when the founder has more experience in the industry ($>10$ years). This is consistent with the human capital theory which argues that experienced entrepreneurs draw on their cognitive maps, network of customers and strategic heuristics to commercialise technological and process innovations more efficiently and so overcome the typical operational challenges that result in early stage failures.

To assess the moderating effect of the Funding Stage ($FS$) of the venture on the relationship between Innovation Capability ($IC$) and Startup Survival ($SS$), Hypothesis H6 was tested. The interaction term ($IC \times FS$) was likewise positive and statistically significant ($\beta = 0.1452$, $t = 4.15$, $p < 0.001$, $f^2 = 0.08$), supporting H6.

This finding reinforces the findings that investing in more advanced stages of funding (seed, Series A, B) does increase the survival returns of innovation capabilities. Easing resource constraints like those of start-ups. Funded startups have the capital runway and opportune slack to create their product and marketing innovations more professional, hire specialized people, and expand. Besides, external funding cushions against the commercialization failures so characteristic of the highly novel innovations that startups must face in their commercialization process.

\subsection{Theoretical Implications}

The structural path results provide robust empirical validation of Dynamic Capabilities Theory and the Resource-Based View (RBV) within the emerging market context. From the RBV perspective, our findings show that Innovation Capability acts as a valuable, rare, and inimitable (VRIN) asset that generates a competitive advantage. In alignment with Dynamic Capabilities Theory, the significant direct and moderated pathways show that early-stage survival is a dynamic process of sensing and seizing opportunities, where founder experience and funding stage act as essential boundary conditions that dictate the efficiency of capability deployment.

\subsection{Practical Implications for the Indian Startup Ecosystem}

For entrepreneurs in the Indian startup ecosystem, our findings suggest that focusing solely on technological development is insufficient; equal attention must be paid to process and marketing innovations to establish a defensible market positioning. Founders must prioritize achieving a distinct market position rather than relying solely on technological novelty. For venture capital and angel investors, the significant moderating effects suggest that when evaluating startup innovation, equal weight must be given to founder human capital and capital deployment efficiency. Finally, for policymakers managing initiatives like the Startup India scheme, institutional support should not only focus on funding but also on building robust mentoring and incubator networks that enrich founders' experience and help them navigate early-stage survival hurdles.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Summary of Objectives and Approach}

This study investigated the critical relationship between multidimensional innovation capability and startup survival among Indian technology ventures. Recognizing the high startup failure rate, we developed a hybrid analytical framework combining PLS-SEM and predictive machine learning. Using survey data from 200 DPIIT-registered startup founders, we mapped the complex path relationships and trained machine learning models to predict survival probabilities based on strategic and operational indicators.

\subsection{Theoretical and Practical Contributions}

The primary contribution of this research is the empirical validation of the mediated and moderated pathways governing startup survival. Theoretically, we demonstrate that competitive advantage acts as a critical mediator, while founder experience and funding stage serve as major amplifiers of the survival benefits of innovation. Practically, this study bridges structural explanations and predictive applications. The serialized Gradient Boosting model serves as the core engine for an interactive self-diagnostic dashboard, enabling startup founders to assess their capabilities, identify weaknesses, and seek targeted advisory resources to mitigate survival risks.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Research}

A primary limitation of this study is its cross-sectional survey design, which restricts our capacity to make direct causal claims over time. Although the sample of 200 startups represents a comprehensive snapshot of the Indian tech ecosystem, future research should employ longitudinal tracking of startups over a five-to-ten-year horizon to capture actual survival and failure events. Additionally, comparative studies across different emerging market ecosystems would be highly valuable to evaluate how regional institutional frameworks influence the relationships identified in our model.
