\section{Conclusion}

This research investigated whether publicly available data provides sufficient information about feature demand to inform product team decision-making. After developing the framework, executing the forecasts, and validating against empirical trends, the evidence supports an affirmative conclusion, subject to appropriate caveats.

Using Notion as the empirical case study, this work demonstrated that signals including search interest, review sentiment, and feature mention patterns contain meaningful information about demand trajectories. When forecasting models were applied to features including Notion AI, templates, and automation workflows, coherent patterns emerged. AI capabilities exhibited the exponential growth trajectory consistent with current technological trends. Templates maintained steady, reliable demand—a foundational feature with consistent user dependence. Automation interest grew more gradually, tracking broader trends in the productivity software sector.

The practical value derives from connecting these forecasts to design decisions. Predicting that "Notion AI interest will grow 30\% next quarter" has limited utility in isolation. The substantive question is what action product teams should take with that information. This framework addresses that question by translating forecast trajectories into recommendations—prioritize this feature, maintain that one, reconsider this other. It serves as a bridge between data science outputs and product management actions.

Honest acknowledgment of limitations is appropriate. This framework does not address all scenarios. Sudden market shifts, unexpected competitive moves, or viral events can invalidate any forecast. Public data serves as a proxy rather than a direct measurement of internal user base activity. The models employed, while sound, have no predictive magic—they perform optimally when historical patterns continue, which is not guaranteed.

Nevertheless, the broader point stands. Proprietary analytics infrastructure is not prerequisite for incorporating data into feature prioritization processes. Smaller teams, startups, and independent researchers can access Google Trends, analyze app reviews, and construct forecasting models. The tools exist. What was previously missing was a framework demonstrating how to integrate these components for SaaS product decisions. That is the contribution this work provides.

Looking forward, demand forecasting is likely to become increasingly routine in software development practice. As the SaaS market becomes more competitive and user expectations continue rising, the ability to anticipate user requirements—rather than merely reacting to current feedback—becomes a competitive advantage. This work aims to provide product teams a foundation for developing that capability.

The essential insight is that understanding future demand is as important as understanding current behavior. By integrating forecasting into the product development cycle, organizations can develop features that anticipate user needs rather than pursuing them retroactively. This represents a more effective approach to software development.
