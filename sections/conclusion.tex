\section{Conclusion}

So what did we actually learn from all this?

The core idea we set out to test was simple: can publicly available data tell us enough about feature demand to help product teams make better decisions? After building the framework, running the forecasts, and validating against real trends, we think the answer is yes—with some caveats.

Using Notion as our case study, we showed that signals like search interest, review sentiment, and feature mention patterns contain real information about where demand is heading. When we applied our forecasting models to features like Notion AI, templates, and automation workflows, we saw patterns that made sense. AI capabilities showed the kind of explosive growth curve you'd expect given the current hype cycle. Templates stayed steady and reliable—a workhorse feature that users depend on. Automation interest grew more gradually, tracking broader trends in the productivity software space.

The practical value comes from connecting these forecasts to design decisions. It's not enough to predict that "Notion AI interest will grow 30\% next quarter." The useful question is: what should a product team actually do with that information? Our framework tries to answer that by translating forecast curves into recommendations—prioritize this, maintain that, reconsider this other thing. It's a bridge between data science outputs and product management actions.

One thing we want to be honest about: this framework doesn't solve everything. Sudden market shifts, unexpected competitor moves, or viral moments can throw off any forecast. Public data is a proxy, not a direct measurement of what's happening inside a company's user base. And the models we used—while solid—aren't magic. They work best when historical patterns continue into the future, which isn't always the case.

Still, the bigger point holds. You don't need proprietary analytics infrastructure to bring data into your feature prioritization process. Smaller teams, startups, independent researchers—anyone can access Google Trends, scrape app reviews, and build forecasting models. The tools are out there. What was missing was a framework that shows how to put the pieces together for SaaS product decisions. That's what we've tried to provide.

Looking ahead, we see demand forecasting becoming a more routine part of how software gets built. As the SaaS market gets more crowded and user expectations keep rising, the ability to anticipate what users will want—rather than just reacting to what they ask for today—becomes a competitive advantage. Our hope is that this work gives product teams a starting point for building that capability themselves.

The bottom line: understanding future demand is just as important as understanding current behavior. By weaving forecasting into the product development cycle, companies can build features that stay ahead of user needs instead of chasing them. That's a better way to build software.
