\section{Related Work}

Before diving into our approach, it helps to see what others have tried. Research on demand forecasting, feature prioritization, and user sentiment analysis has covered a lot of ground—but often in isolation. Here's what the existing work looks like and where the gaps are.

\subsection{Forecasting Demand for Software Features}
Most forecasting research grew out of predicting demand for physical products—shoes, electronics, groceries. The ARIMA models that Box and Jenkins developed decades ago still get used everywhere because they're solid and well-understood \cite{box2015time}. But software features aren't like physical products. They don't follow neat seasonal curves. A feature can explode in popularity overnight because someone tweeted about it, or tank because a competitor launched something better.

Researchers noticed that Google search trends actually correlate pretty well with real-world interest. People have used search data to predict everything from stock prices to flu outbreaks \cite{choi2012predicting}. The idea that search behavior signals genuine demand makes sense—when people want something, they search for it \cite{jun2014examining}. This gave us confidence that publicly available search data could tell us something useful about feature demand.

\subsection{How SaaS Teams Prioritize Features Today}
Product managers have their frameworks: RICE scoring, Kano models, value-versus-effort matrices \cite{kang1990feature}. These tools help organize thinking, but they mostly rely on subjective judgment. Someone has to estimate impact, guess at user value, or rank features based on internal discussions. That's fine when you have a small product, but it doesn't scale well and it's prone to bias.

Some companies have moved toward analytics-driven prioritization, using internal usage data to inform roadmap decisions. But here's the problem for researchers: that data is proprietary. You can't study it, replicate findings, or generalize lessons to other products. Our work tries to sidestep this by showing that publicly available data—which anyone can access—carries enough signal to inform feature decisions.

\subsection{Time Series Methods and Machine Learning}
When it comes to actually building forecasting models, there's a spectrum of approaches. On one end, you have classical statistical methods like Exponential Smoothing and ARIMA \cite{hyndman2008forecasting}. They're interpretable, well-tested, and don't need mountains of data to work. On the other end, machine learning models—neural networks, gradient boosting, random forests—can capture complex patterns but often need more data and are harder to explain.

What's interesting is that simpler models often hold their own against fancier alternatives, especially when you don't have huge datasets \cite{makridakis2018statistical}. For our use case—predicting interest in specific software features using public data—a blend of classical techniques made more sense than throwing deep learning at the problem. Recent work on scalable forecasting \cite{taylor2018forecasting} has also shown that hybrid approaches combining statistical rigor with practical deployment can yield excellent results.

\subsection{Understanding What Users Actually Think}
App store reviews and social media discussions are goldmines for understanding user sentiment \cite{cantallops2014new, pang2008opinion}. Natural language processing lets you scan thousands of reviews and extract what people like, what frustrates them, and which features they keep mentioning \cite{chen2014ar, pagano2013user}. Sentiment analysis adds another layer: not just what people talk about, but how they feel about it \cite{guzman2014users}.

Most existing work in this space focuses on analyzing what users think about features that already exist \cite{maalej2016automatic}. Our angle is slightly different—we're trying to predict future demand, not just measure current opinions. But sentiment trends over time can hint at where demand is heading, so we incorporated this into our approach.

\subsection{Where We Fit In}
The gap we're addressing is straightforward: there's solid research on forecasting, solid research on feature prioritization, and solid research on sentiment analysis—but not much that ties them together for SaaS product design. Forecasting papers focus on traditional products. Prioritization frameworks stay qualitative. Sentiment work looks backward more than forward.

We built a framework that pulls these threads together. By combining multiple forecasting techniques, validating against real feature trends, and using only publicly available data, we show a practical path that product teams can actually follow \cite{licorish2017exploring, berger2020managers}. The goal isn't academic novelty—it's giving smaller teams access to the kind of data-driven decision-making that usually requires a big analytics operation.
