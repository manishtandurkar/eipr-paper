\section{Related Work}

This section reviews existing literature relevant to the proposed framework, examining prior research in demand forecasting, feature prioritization, time series analysis, and sentiment analysis. The review identifies gaps in current approaches that this work aims to address.

\subsection{Demand Forecasting for Software Features}
Traditional demand forecasting research originated in contexts involving physical products—consumer goods, electronics, and retail inventory. The ARIMA models developed by Box and Jenkins continue to be widely applied due to their robustness and interpretability \cite{box2015time}. However, software features exhibit distinct characteristics that differentiate them from physical products. Feature demand does not follow predictable seasonal patterns and may experience rapid fluctuations in response to viral content, competitive announcements, or shifts in user behavior.

Research has demonstrated that internet search trends correlate significantly with real-world interest patterns. Studies have utilized search data to predict phenomena ranging from financial market movements to epidemiological trends \cite{choi2012predicting}. The underlying principle—that search behavior reflects genuine user interest—provides theoretical justification for using publicly available search data as a demand proxy \cite{jun2014examining}. This insight informed the decision to incorporate Google Trends data as a primary demand signal in this framework.

\subsection{Feature Prioritization in SaaS Products}
Product management practitioners employ various prioritization frameworks including RICE scoring, Kano models, and value-versus-effort matrices \cite{kang1990feature}. While these frameworks provide structured approaches to decision-making, they rely substantially on subjective judgment. Practitioners must estimate impact, assess user value, and rank features based on internal discussions. Such approaches function adequately for simple products but scale poorly and remain susceptible to cognitive biases.

Some organizations have adopted analytics-driven prioritization methodologies utilizing internal usage data to inform roadmap decisions. However, this internal data remains proprietary and inaccessible to external researchers, limiting the ability to study, replicate, or generalize findings across products. This research addresses this limitation by demonstrating that publicly available data contains sufficient signal strength to inform feature prioritization decisions.

\subsection{Time Series Methods and Machine Learning}
Forecasting methodologies span a spectrum from classical statistical methods to contemporary machine learning approaches. Classical techniques including Exponential Smoothing and ARIMA models \cite{hyndman2008forecasting} offer interpretability, established theoretical foundations, and function effectively with limited data. Machine learning approaches—neural networks, gradient boosting, random forests—can capture complex non-linear patterns but typically require larger datasets and reduced interpretability.

Comparative studies indicate that simpler models frequently achieve competitive performance against more complex alternatives, particularly with limited datasets \cite{makridakis2018statistical}. For the present use case—predicting interest in specific software features using public data—an ensemble of classical techniques provides an appropriate balance of accuracy and interpretability. Recent work on scalable forecasting \cite{taylor2018forecasting} has demonstrated that hybrid approaches combining statistical rigor with practical deployment considerations yield excellent results.

\subsection{Sentiment Analysis and User Feedback}
App store reviews and social media discussions constitute valuable sources for understanding user sentiment \cite{cantallops2014new, pang2008opinion}. Natural language processing techniques enable analysis of large review corpora to extract feature mentions, pain points, and satisfaction patterns \cite{chen2014ar, pagano2013user}. Sentiment analysis provides additional context regarding not only what users discuss but their affective orientation toward specific features \cite{guzman2014users}.

Previous work in this domain has focused primarily on analyzing current user opinions regarding existing features \cite{maalej2016automatic}. This research differs by attempting to predict future demand rather than merely measuring current sentiment. However, temporal trends in sentiment can indicate demand trajectory, justifying the incorporation of sentiment analysis within the forecasting framework.

\subsection{Research Gap and Contribution}
The gap addressed by this research is the absence of integrated frameworks connecting forecasting, prioritization, and sentiment analysis for SaaS product design. Forecasting research has traditionally focused on physical products. Prioritization frameworks remain qualitative in nature. Sentiment analysis work is primarily retrospective rather than predictive.

This work develops a framework integrating these research streams. By combining multiple forecasting techniques, validating against empirical feature trends, and utilizing exclusively publicly available data, this research demonstrates a practical approach accessible to product teams without extensive analytical infrastructure \cite{licorish2017exploring, berger2020managers}. The contribution is methodological rather than algorithmic—providing smaller teams access to data-driven decision-making capabilities typically requiring substantial analytical resources.
