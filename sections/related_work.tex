\section{Related Work}

The intersection of demand forecasting, SaaS analytics, and product design has attracted considerable research attention across multiple domains. This section reviews relevant work in demand forecasting methodologies, feature prioritization in software products, and the application of time series analysis to user behavior prediction.

\subsection{Demand Forecasting in Software Products}
Traditional demand forecasting has primarily focused on physical goods and retail environments. Box and Jenkins' seminal work on ARIMA models established foundational time series techniques that remain widely used today \cite{box2015time}. However, forecasting demand for digital features presents unique challenges. Unlike physical products, software features exhibit rapid adoption curves, network effects, and sensitivity to external triggers such as competitor releases or viral social media trends. Recent studies have explored how search trends correlate with product interest, with Google Trends emerging as a valuable proxy for consumer behavior \cite{choi2012predicting}. Researchers have successfully applied these techniques to predict stock market movements, disease outbreaks, and consumer electronics demand, demonstrating the viability of publicly available search data for forecasting purposes.

\subsection{Feature Prioritization in SaaS}
Product management literature emphasizes various frameworks for feature prioritization, including RICE scoring (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort), Kano models, and value-vs-effort matrices \cite{wiegers2013software}. However, these methods typically rely on subjective assessments or require extensive user research. More recent approaches incorporate analytics-driven decision making, where companies leverage internal usage data to inform roadmap decisions \cite{olsson2014climbing}. The challenge for academic research lies in the proprietary nature of such data, limiting the reproducibility and generalizability of findings. Our work addresses this gap by demonstrating that publicly accessible datasets can provide meaningful demand signals without compromising user privacy or requiring insider access.

\subsection{Time Series Analysis and Machine Learning}
The application of time series forecasting to technology adoption has been explored through various lenses. Exponential Smoothing methods, particularly Holt-Winters models, have proven effective for capturing seasonal patterns and trends in user behavior \cite{hyndman2008forecasting}. ARIMA and SARIMA models offer robust statistical foundations for understanding autocorrelation in temporal data. More recently, machine learning approaches including regression models with engineered features, neural networks, and ensemble methods have gained traction for their ability to capture nonlinear relationships \cite{james2013introduction}. Studies comparing these techniques often find that simpler statistical models perform competitively with complex machine learning approaches, especially when training data is limited or when interpretability is valued.

\subsection{User Sentiment and Review Analysis}
Understanding user sentiment toward software features has become increasingly important in product development. Natural language processing techniques applied to app store reviews and social media discussions can reveal feature requests, usability issues, and satisfaction levels \cite{guzman2014users}. Sentiment analysis combined with temporal trends provides insights into how feature perception evolves over time. However, most existing work focuses on post-launch analysis rather than predictive forecasting, analyzing what users think about existing features rather than anticipating future demand for planned capabilities.

\subsection{Gap Analysis and Contribution}
While substantial work exists in isolated areas—demand forecasting, feature prioritization, and sentiment analysis—few studies integrate these approaches specifically for SaaS product design. Most demand forecasting research focuses on traditional products, while feature prioritization frameworks remain largely qualitative. Our work bridges these gaps by developing a quantitative forecasting framework tailored to SaaS environments, using Notion as a concrete case study. By combining multiple forecasting techniques and validating predictions against real-world feature launches and adoption patterns, we demonstrate a practical methodology that product teams can implement using only publicly available data. This approach democratizes data-driven product management, making sophisticated forecasting accessible to teams without extensive analytics infrastructure.
