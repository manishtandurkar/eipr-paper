\section{Introduction}

These days, SaaS tools have become part of daily life for millions of people—whether they're managing personal tasks, collaborating with teammates, or running entire digital workflows. Among the crowd, Notion has carved out a special spot. It's flexible, modular, and keeps adding new tricks like AI-powered features. But as the platform grows, so does the problem of deciding what to build next. Product teams can't just go with their gut or rely on a handful of user complaints. They need a smarter way to figure out what users actually want before spending months building something nobody asked for.

That's where demand forecasting comes in. Instead of guessing, you look at the signals—what people are searching for, what they're saying in reviews, how their behavior shifts over time—and use that data to predict which features will matter most in the coming months. For SaaS products, this is trickier than forecasting demand for, say, sneakers or refrigerators. Software feature interest doesn't follow neat seasonal patterns. It can spike overnight because of a viral tweet, a competitor's launch, or a sudden shift in remote work habits.

This study digs into how forecasting can guide product design decisions, using Notion as a real-world case study. We focus on publicly available data since internal analytics from companies like Notion aren't exactly floating around for researchers to use. By looking at trends in Google search interest, app store reviews, and user feedback, we show how forecasting models can highlight which features are heating up and which ones are cooling off. The ultimate goal isn't just to predict demand—it's to connect those predictions to actual design choices, like improving a feature's UI, reshuffling priorities, or planning release timing.

\subsection{Why This Matters}
SaaS productivity platforms operate in a constantly shifting landscape. User expectations evolve quickly, new workflow patterns pop up all the time, and competitors are always breathing down your neck. Getting the timing right on features can make or break a product's success. Since we can't access Notion's internal dashboards, we built a forecasting method that relies on publicly available proxies—Google Trends for search interest, Kaggle datasets for reviews, and app metadata for ratings and download counts. It's not perfect, but it's a practical starting point for any team that wants to bring data into their design process.

\subsection{The Problem We're Tackling}
Product teams at SaaS companies constantly wrestle with prioritization. Which feature should we improve? What should we build next? Without comprehensive analytics, they often fall back on intuition or squeaky-wheel feedback—whoever complains loudest gets attention. That's not a great way to run things. There's a real need for a data-driven approach that can anticipate what users will want and feed that insight into design decisions before it's too late.

\subsection{What We Set Out to Do}
Our objectives for this research were:
\begin{itemize}
\item Understand how interest in different Notion features shifts over time by analyzing publicly available data like Google Trends and user reviews.
\item Apply forecasting methods—Exponential Smoothing, ARIMA, and Regression—to predict which features are likely to gain or lose traction.
\item Explore how those predictions can guide real product decisions: what to prioritize, what to improve, what to reconsider.
\item Build a practical framework that software teams can actually use to connect demand insights with UI/UX design and release planning.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scope and Why It Matters}
We're not claiming this framework solves every forecasting problem in the SaaS world. But we do aim to show that even without fancy internal analytics, teams can build useful demand predictions from public data. The bigger picture here is that effective forecasting helps companies build better features, keep pace with user needs, reduce churn, and improve adoption. We focus on Notion as our case study, but the approach should translate to other SaaS platforms with a bit of adaptation.
